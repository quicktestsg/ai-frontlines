Done Is a Contract, Not a Feeling

Ask an agent whether its work is finished and the answer is yes. Ask whether it is good and the answer is also yes. The model's confidence is a statement about the fluency of its own text, not a measurement of the artifact. The fix predates agents by centuries: agree on what 'done' means before the work starts, in writing, between two parties with opposed incentives.

Watch what happens in the standard single-agent harness when the model decides it is finished. It has just produced four thousand tokens of implementation, its context is warm with everything it wrote, and the harness asks: are you done? The question is being answered by the entity with the most complete knowledge of the effort expended and the least incentive to audit it. Model confidence is not a measurement — it is the model reporting how likely its own continuation feels to itself. When the model that wrote the feature also grades it, the grader shares the builder's blind spots, because they are the same weights. Whatever the builder did not think to implement, the grader did not think to test. The two mistakes are one mistake.

The pattern that fixes this has been rediscovered in enough places that it deserves to be treated as a law rather than a technique. In software teams it is acceptance criteria written before the code. In contract law it is the specification that precedes performance. In adversarial machine learning it is the discriminator that never sees the generator's self-assessment, only its outputs. The common shape: quality is defined before the work, by a party that did not do the work, in terms checkable without anyone's opinion. Ported to agent harnesses, this becomes a sprint contract: the builder proposes what 'done' means, a separate evaluator attacks the proposal until every criterion is binary and checkable against the running artifact, the contract is signed, and the verdict arrives per clause.

Two harnesses and the moment 'done' is decided. In the self-grading harness, the builder writes the code and then judges it, so confidence about its own text substitutes for a measurement of the artifact. In the contract harness, a separate evaluator attacks the proposed definition of done before any code exists; building starts only under a signed, binary-verifiable contract, and the verdict comes from executing that contract against the running system — clause by clause, with hard thresholds.

What makes a criterion contract-grade

Not every sentence about quality can survive contact with a contract. 'The UI should look polished' is a feeling wearing a clause's clothes. A contract-grade criterion is binary ('the main view renders within two seconds on a cold start, measured by the harness clock'), verifiable from outside — by a party that cannot read the builder's mind, via a browser session, an API response, a database row, a log line — and it names its error path. Error paths are where self-graded agents hide their failures, because error paths produce no tokens unless something forces the issue.

The negotiation, clause by clause: the evaluator rewrites every feeling-shaped sentence into a binary, world-verifiable check. 'Polished' becomes a render-time budget measured by the harness clock. 'Graceful error handling' becomes the exact observable state after an expired card: error present, cart intact, zero rows written. 'Robust' becomes a 4xx with an error schema and no stack trace. A clause that cannot be executed against the running system is not a clause — it is a hope, and hopes do not gate sprints.

Contracts can be gamed — design for it

The honest objection is Goodhart's law: once a measure becomes a target, the builder optimizes the measure, not the quality behind it. Three design choices blunt this. The evaluator holds the pen — the signed version of the criteria is authored by the party that executes them, and the builder sees the category of test, never the instance. Criteria are sampled, not exhaustive — the contract specifies the space of checks, and instances are drawn at evaluation time, so hardcoding one passing case buys nothing. And the verdict is thresholded per clause with a hard floor — one clause below the bar fails the sprint regardless of the others — removing the averaging that lets a builder trade one dazzling clause against three hollow ones. It is not ungameable; nothing is. It moves the cost of gaming above the cost of building properly, which is all a contract has ever done.

A spec written after the work is a press release. A spec written before the work is a load-bearing structure. Same words, opposite physics — the only difference is who was committed, and when.

The economics deserve a number: contract harnesses cost more, and the extra spend must be justified by the failure distribution, not aesthetics. A solo agent produces a plausible feature in twenty minutes for pocket change; the contract harness may run hours and cost an order of magnitude more, most of it burned by an evaluator clicking through the running application the way a user would. The exchange rate: unverified tokens for verified states. For toy work, narration is fine. For work someone will stand behind — a migration, a payment flow, anything with a rollback cost — the contract is cheaper than the incident, every time. The boundary is drifting inward as evaluator agents get cheaper: verification is becoming the commodity, and the scarce skill is authoring criteria worth verifying.

The exchange rate between the two harnesses. A solo run is fast and cheap and yields a narrated probability of working — the author's testimony. A contract run is slower and pricier and yields witnessed behaviors: clauses executed against the running system by a party with no stake in the narrative. The rule is a cost comparison — pay for the contract whenever a post-ship defect costs more than the pre-build negotiation — and as evaluator agents get cheaper, the boundary keeps drifting inward.

The deepest change is not to any single run but to the boundaries between agents. Today's multi-agent systems pass artifacts across those boundaries like hot potatoes, each consumer trusting the producer's word. Contracts turn every handoff into a negotiation and every artifact into a claim pre-attached to its own falsification procedure. The planner decomposes not just the work but the definition of done, and each subcontract inherits clauses it did not write and cannot weaken. That is the architecture worth stealing: not three agents, but one pen, deliberately placed in the hands of whoever does not benefit from the lie. Humans built this arrangement out of mutual distrust and called it civilization. Agents get to skip the millennia of litigation and start from the good part. Done is a contract. Everything else is a feeling, and feelings do not deploy.
