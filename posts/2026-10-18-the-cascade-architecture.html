The Cascade: Architecture of Affordable Intelligence

An agent loop multiplies tokens. A fifty-step run at frontier-model prices is not a rounding error — it is a line item that decides whether the product survives.

The economics of agents are brutal in a way that chatbots never had to face. A chatbot makes one model call per question. An agent makes one per step — and a step is cheap to take and easy to multiply.

Most teams discover this in production, when the invoice arrives. Then they swing to the other extreme: swap the frontier model for the cheapest fast model in every step, and watch quality collapse.

A triage node estimates the difficulty of each step and routes it to a tier. Every tier's output passes a verifier. A pass is emitted; a failure escalates one tier up, carrying the failed attempt as context. The expensive model is not the default — it is the last resort, reached only on evidence.

The step is the unit of accounting

The first move in cascade design is to stop treating the run as an indivisible whole. Look at a real trace of a coding agent and count what the model is actually doing. In a representative fifty-step run, a large share of steps are mechanical: re-reading a file, formatting a tool argument, deciding which of three tools to call. These steps barely need intelligence at all — they need reliability, speed, and price.

This is also why the cascade belongs in the graph, not in the prompt. Telling a single all-purpose model to 'think briefly when the task is easy' is a wish, not a mechanism. A cascade makes the decision structural: the triage node is a node, the tiers are nodes, the escalation edges are edges with conditions attached.

A cascade is not a hack around model prices. It is the admission that pricing is a routing problem — and routing is an engineering discipline. The model providers sell intelligence by the token. The engineering team's job is to decide which tokens are worth buying.

Escalation is not failure

The heart of the cascade is the escalation edge, and the heart of the escalation edge is the verifier. Something must decide that tier 1's answer is not good enough — before the answer reaches the user or the next step consumes it.

Escalation should also carry its history. When a step escalates from tier 1 to tier 2, the higher tier should receive the failed attempt and the verifier's reason for rejecting it. This does two things. It makes the expensive call cheaper, because the strong model does not re-derive what the weak model already established — it debugs it.

The escalation edge in detail. A structural verifier — schemas, compilers, tests, not model opinions — decides pass or fail. A pass is emitted and logged. A failure escalates with its history attached, so the stronger tier debugs instead of re-deriving. Every escalation is simultaneously labeled data for improving the triage node itself.

The frontier region is narrow — and it moves

Plot task difficulty against required model tier and an uncomfortable picture emerges. The region where the frontier model is actually necessary is narrow: tasks hard enough to defeat mid-tier models, but tractable enough to be solvable at all.

Quality against difficulty for three tiers. On the left, cheap tiers saturate: quality is flat while price climbs, and the frontier premium buys nothing. On the right, every tier collapses. The frontier model earns its price only inside the narrow band in between.

The band also moves. Every model release shifts it: today's frontier capability becomes next quarter's mid-tier default, and the wall of unsolvability creeps right. This is why a cascade must be treated as a living routing table, not a one-time decision.

Give every edge a budget

The final piece of the architecture is the one most often skipped: a budget attribute on the graph itself. Every node declares what it is allowed to spend — in tokens, in calls, in wall-clock time — and the runtime enforces it.

Put together — triage nodes, escalation edges with verifiers, a mapped frontier band, budgets on every node — the cascade reframes what an agent even is. An agent is not a model with tools bolted on. It is a routing system for intelligence.
