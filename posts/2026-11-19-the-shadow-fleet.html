The Shadow Fleet

Classic software ships behind feature flags and canary percentages, and it works because the software is deterministic. An agent is not. The teams that ship weekly run a shadow fleet: the candidate serves a silent copy of live traffic, answers discarded, outcomes diffed, promotion earned with evidence.

There is a habit left over from a decade of microservice releases that dies hard in agent engineering: treat a model swap like a library upgrade. Every step of that ritual assumes the thing being deployed has one behavior. Agents break that assumption at its root. A 1% canary doesn't sample a distribution — it samples one draw from it, and the dashboards average away exactly the variance you needed to see.

Every live request is teed: production answers the user, while the candidate build processes a sampled copy against a mirrored world. A differ compares outcomes across both fleets: agreement rate, trajectory length, tool mix, cost. The shadow's output is discarded. The evidence it produces is not.

Compare outcomes, not tokens

The first mistake every team makes with shadow traffic is diffing the text. The candidate will phrase things differently, take the steps in a different order, choose a different tool for the same subgoal — and a naive diff flags all of it as change, burying the signal in noise within a day. The diff that matters is the diff of outcomes. Once outcomes are the unit of comparison, the noise collapses and four quadrants emerge — and each quadrant carries a different instruction for the release.

Every shadowed task lands in one of four quadrants. Agreement means promote. Shadow-only wins mean the candidate is fixing real production failures. Prod-only wins are regressions on tasks users already rely on — exactly the fires a percentage rollout averages away. Both-fail is not a release problem at all; it is a free, continuously refreshed map of where the product is weakest.

The shadow world must be hermetic

The shadow fleet has one non-negotiable rule: it can never touch the real world. A shadow agent that sends the email, charges the card, or writes to the production database is not a shadow — it is a duplicate user with a model behind it. The shadow runs against a mirror: forked state, sandboxed writes, stubbed externals. The mirror needs to be fresh enough that trajectories are representative, and isolated enough that a rogue shadow can do no harm.

Promotion is a ramp, not a switch

When the shadow has earned its keep — agreement high, regressions at zero, cost per task flat — the candidate still does not jump to full traffic. The shadow proved the candidate on a mirrored world; the ramp proves it on the real one. The canary is just the shadow with the mute button off, and the rollback threshold is just the differ's alarm wired to the deployment switch. One measurement system, two uses, zero new failure modes.

The candidate climbs a ramp of increasing exposure: fully sampled shadow, then 1% live, then 10%, then everything — and each gate reuses the metrics the differ already computed. Rollback is not a separate mechanism; it is the differ's alarm with the authority to demote the candidate back down the ramp.

What the shadow cannot tell you

Honesty about the limits keeps the practice alive. Shadows measure the delta between two builds on today's traffic; they say nothing about the traffic of next month. A mirror that drifts from production state quietly degrades the evidence. Timing-dependent side effects are invisible by design. And shadows cost money — every sampled request is a second full agent run, so sampling rates become a budget line. None of this breaks the method. It defines it: not a proof of safety, but the cheapest evidence machine a team can build.

A canary answers one question: does the new build crash? A shadow fleet answers the question that actually matters: does the new build behave — on real requests, against real outcomes, without anyone paying for its mistakes. The teams that ship agents every week are not braver than the teams that ship every quarter. They just replaced hope with a second fleet.

Start smaller than feels useful. Pick one high-traffic task type, mirror its world crudely, sample one request in fifty, and tee it to a candidate build that differs from production by exactly one variable. Run it for a week. The first outcome diff will be humbling — and the slow realization that the team never knew how variable its agent was. Once the variance is visible, it can be budgeted, bounded, and improved. The fleet is not infrastructure. It is a culture of refusing to ship blind, expressed in servers.
