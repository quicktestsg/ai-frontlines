Your Agent Is a Daemon Now

The first generation of agents was built like functions. The generation now arriving is built like daemons — always resident, watching, acting on their own schedule. Five decades of OS engineering already wrote the survival playbook for that. The agent world just hasn't read it.

There is a quiet architectural shift underway in how agents are deployed. The agent of the last two years was a function: it existed only inside a request. The new agent is a daemon. It starts when the system starts, subscribes to mailboxes and repositories and logs, holds long-lived memory and long-lived credentials, and does not wait to be invoked.

Top lane: the invoke-once agent, whose every failure is bounded by the request that contained it. Bottom lane: the resident agent, whose failures are unbounded by any request — and whose judgment about when to act becomes the most load-bearing decision in the system.

Daemons don't fail like functions

A function has three failure modes: wrong answer, timeout, crash. All visible at the call site. A daemon fails differently: by drift, by accumulation, by wakefulness, and — worst of all — by silence, when it quietly stops doing the thing it was supposed to keep doing.

The question that defines function-era engineering is 'how do I make this answer better?' The question that defines daemon-era engineering is 'how do I make this process survivable?' Borrowing the first to answer the second is why ambient agents keep embarrassing their makers.

Here is the good news: this problem was solved once before, in the 1970s, by people managing timesharing systems. Unix daemons have run for fifty years, and the OS community developed a precise, unfashionable toolkit for keeping them honest. It has four instruments, and each translates almost mechanically to agent engineering.

Four instruments from fifty years of daemon engineering: a scheduler decides what the agent attends to; interrupt coalescing decides when the human is woken; a watchdog bounds runaway loops; least privilege ensures the blast radius was decided by the designer, not by the agent in the moment.

Attention is a scheduler, not a feed

The deep mistake in most ambient agent designs is treating attention as a feed. Feeds are honest when volume is low and dishonest forever after, because a feed has no notion of priority — only of recency. An ambient agent should hold a prioritized queue of candidate actions, re-sort it every time it wakes, and treat 'do nothing' as a first-class scheduling outcome. The daemon that acts on everything is not diligent. It is a busy loop wearing a personality.

Interrupt coalescing is the piece that most directly protects the human. In an ambient agent system, the expensive resource is the human's focus, and the context switch is the notification. Per-event pings are not responsiveness — they are the agent outsourcing its scheduling problem to the least scalable component in the system.

Supervision, the Unix way: the agent emits heartbeats; a separate, simpler watchdog kills the run that hung; a decision journal records every autonomous act before execution, so restart is recovery rather than amnesia.

Quiet is a feature

The hardest culture shift is aesthetic, not technical. Invoke-once agents were judged by liveliness; resident agents must be judged by restraint. A well-scheduled daemon is mostly idle, and most days correctly end with nothing to report. The systems that earn years of unattended trust will not be the ones that acted brilliantly once. They will be the ones that knew, ten thousand times in a row, when not to.
