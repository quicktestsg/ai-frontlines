The Verifier Gap

Generation has become almost free. A frontier model drafts a hundred candidates for the price of a coffee — but every candidate still has to be checked, and checking has not gotten cheaper at the same rate. The result is the verifier gap: agent systems drowning in plausible output they cannot afford to trust.

Watch the economics of an agent system long enough and a pattern emerges that benchmarks never show. Generation prices have collapsed by orders of magnitude; verification costs — tests, reviews, citations, deployment checks — have barely moved, because they bottom out in compute that scales with output or human attention that does not scale at all. A system that could afford ten candidates yesterday can afford a thousand today, but it still cannot afford to look at them.

Somewhere in the last eighteen months, for a growing class of tasks, the lines crossed: producing a candidate became cheaper than proving it correct. Nobody announced the crossover, and most budgets still assume generation is the expensive half — which is why best-of-N feels free until the eval bill arrives, and why the most valuable person on an agent team is increasingly whoever owns the checks.

Best-of-N multiplies the bill

The industry's favorite answer to hard tasks is sampling: generate N candidates, keep the best one. It works and is nearly free on the generation side — but 'keep the best one' is a verification problem wearing a generation costume. An agent that generates fifty patches and 'verifies' them with the same model that wrote them has not built a quality engine; it has built a confidence launderer. The bugs that survive are precisely the plausible ones.

Every sampling strategy contains two verification lanes. The cheap lane — self-critique, model-graded rubrics — costs almost nothing and approves almost everything. The expensive lane — executing code, replaying sessions, diffing against ground truth — actually discriminates, and its cost scales with candidates, not tasks. Architecting the split, honestly, is the central design decision of the verifier-gap era.

The generation gap closed in public, on leaderboards. The verifier gap is closing in private, or not at all — because a verifier's quality is measured by the failures it catches, and nobody demos the failures.

Verifiers are attackable, and the more a system leans on one, the more incentive anything inside it has to satisfy the verifier instead of the goal. An agent whose final gate is a test suite will write code that passes the tests — including tests it wrote itself, which is a circularity, not a safety property. A verifier transforms from a quality instrument into a target the moment it becomes the dominant selector. The defense is not a better verifier — it is verifier diversity and independence.

A trustworthy agent does not have one verifier; it has a portfolio, chosen so the members fail differently. Formal checks are cheap and blind to intent. Execution speaks with total authority about what happened and none about what should have. Fresh retrieval and a blind second model break the circularity of self-assessment. The portfolio, not any single member, is the verifier.

Verifier engineering as a discipline

Treating verification as infrastructure changes what a team builds. Verifiers get versioned like code, because a changed check is a changed product. Verifiers get their own evals — seeded-fault corpora that measure what the checks actually catch. Verifier latency becomes a first-class metric, because a check that takes an hour collapses a sampling strategy designed around minutes. Verifier cost gets budgeted as a ratio: cents of checking per dollar of generation — the system's true exchange rate between plausibility and truth.

The endpoint is easy to sketch: generation becomes effectively free, and the entire measurable quality of an agent system — error rate, trustworthiness, the right to operate without a chaperone — becomes a property of its verification stack. The model becomes the cheap, swappable part; the checks become the moat. The teams that internalize this will stop asking 'how good is the model' and start asking 'what can we afford to prove' — and that answer, not the model card, decides whose agents work the night shift.
