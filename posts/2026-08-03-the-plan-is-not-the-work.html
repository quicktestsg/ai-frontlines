The Plan Is Not the Work

An agent writes a plan, and the team leans toward the screen. The plan is brilliant — five crisp steps, each reasonable, the whole arc elegant. Then execution begins, and the plan starts dying on contact with reality. The plan was always the cheap part.

Watch a team demo an agent for the first time, and they will almost always show you the plan. The user asks something hard. There is a pause. Then the agent emits a structured plan — a numbered list, sometimes a tree, sometimes a sequence of sub-tasks with dependencies. The steps are sensible. The ordering is logical. The team points at it and says: look, it thinks. This is the moment that sells agent systems, because planning is the most legible form of intelligence. A plan is something a human recognizes as intelligence, because humans plan the same way — break the problem down, order the pieces, then do them. So the team concludes the agent can plan, and by extension, can do the task. This conclusion is the most expensive mistake in agent engineering.

The plan on the left is what the team demos. It costs a single model call and contains zero information the model did not already have. The execution on the right is what actually runs. Every step the plan assumed was simple turns into a sub-tree of unanticipated recovery work. The plan is a hypothesis. Execution is the experiment that tests it — and experiments are where the cost, the latency, and the failures live.

Planning is the easy part

This needs to be said plainly, because the entire industry has decided that planning is the hard, impressive part of agent behavior. It is not. Planning is, in fact, the single easiest thing a frontier model does. Ask any capable model to break a task into steps, and it will — fluently, reasonably, almost every time. This is not because the model is a brilliant strategist. It is because planning, as produced by a language model, is pattern completion. The model has seen millions of decomposed tasks in its training data. When asked to plan, it produces the shape of a plan — the steps, the ordering, the dependencies — by matching the most common structure for that kind of task. The plan is statistically likely to look right. Looking right and being right are not the same thing, but the team watching the demo cannot tell the difference, because the difference only appears during execution.

Consider what a plan actually is. It is a prediction about the future — a claim that the task decomposes into these specific steps, that step two will produce the inputs step three needs, that the tools will behave as expected, that nothing along the way will surprise the agent into revision. A plan is a forecast. And like all forecasts, it is only as good as the assumptions it rests on. The problem is that the model makes the plan before it has tested any of those assumptions. It is planning blind. It does not know what the API will return, whether the data will be in the expected format, whether the third step's premise survives contact with the second step's results. The plan is written in a state of maximum ignorance and minimum cost. Every step of execution adds information and removes ignorance — but also has the power to invalidate the plan that was written without that information.

A plan is a prediction made in the dark. Execution is the light. Most agent engineering teams spend their time polishing the prediction and almost none designing what happens when the light turns on and the room looks different from what was expected.

The mismatch is almost universal. Teams pour effort into making the plan look better — richer planning prompts, structured plan formats, reflection on the plan. But the plan rarely fails. What fails is the execution: the step that breaks, the assumption that was wrong, the moment the agent must decide whether to push through a broken plan or revise it. That decision — and the recovery machinery behind it — is where the failures live, and it is where almost no effort goes.

The brittle plan and the flexible plan

Not all plans are equal, and the difference between a good plan and a bad one is not whether the steps are correct — no plan gets every step right. The difference is what the plan permits when it turns out to be wrong. A brittle plan is one that the agent treats as a contract: it executes step one, then step two, then step three, in sequence, and if step two produces something unexpected, the agent either ignores it and proceeds, or halts entirely. The brittle plan has no mechanism for revision. It was expensive to produce, so the agent commits to it, and the commitment becomes the failure mode. A flexible plan is one that the agent treats as a draft: it is a starting point, a set of hypotheses to be tested by execution, and every step's result is allowed to change what comes next. The flexible plan is cheap to abandon and cheap to rewrite, because it was never meant to survive unchanged.

This distinction maps onto a concrete engineering choice, and it is the choice that determines whether an agent degrades gracefully or catastrophically. The brittle agent has a plan phase and an execution phase, separated cleanly: plan once, then execute the plan. The flexible agent interleaves them: plan a little, execute a little, observe, re-plan. The interleaved agent is harder to build — the loop is more complex, the bookkeeping is messier, the control flow is not a straight line. But it fails better. When a step's result contradicts the plan, the interleaved agent folds that information into the next planning moment, producing a revised plan that accounts for what just happened. The brittle agent has no next planning moment. It planned once, at the start, and now it is trapped inside that plan, executing steps whose premises have already been disproven.

The brittle agent on top commits to a plan and executes it as a contract. When step three's premise is disproved by step two's results, there is no mechanism to notice or respond — the agent runs the broken steps to completion. The team that stops optimizing the plan

Here is what changes when a team internalizes that the plan is not the work. They stop spending days refining the planning prompt. They stop adding elaborate plan-validation steps that check whether the plan is internally consistent — because internal consistency is cheap and irrelevant when the plan's premises are about to be tested by tools that return unexpected results. They stop treating the plan as a deliverable, as the thing to show stakeholders, as proof the agent is smart. Instead, they invest in three things that actually matter, and the first is the re-plan trigger. A plan is only as good as the system's ability to detect when it has gone wrong. This means checking, after every step, whether the result is consistent with what the plan assumed. The check does not need to be elaborate. A simple comparison — does the tool output match the shape the plan expected? — catches most failures. The teams that win build these checks cheaply and place them frequently, so that a broken premise is caught after one step, not after five.

The second is cheap re-planning. If re-planning is expensive — a full model call with the entire history, a heavy re-derivation of the whole plan — the system will avoid it, and the agent will push through broken plans because revising is too costly. The fix is to make re-planning local: revise only the affected steps, not the whole plan, and feed the agent only what changed. The third is the exit. Every plan needs a point where the system decides the task is done, or decides it cannot be done, and stops. Teams that focus on the plan often forget this, and their agents either loop forever revising a plan that will never work, or declare success after executing a plan whose results were never checked against the actual goal. The exit is not part of the plan. It is part of the execution architecture, and it is the thing that keeps a broken plan from becoming an infinite one.

None of these three are part of the plan itself. They are part of the execution architecture — the machinery that runs the plan, tests it against reality, and decides what to do when the plan and reality disagree. A team that invests in the re-plan trigger, cheap local re-planning, and a real exit condition has built an agent that survives its own wrong assumptions. A team that invests only in making prettier plans has built an agent that fails the moment reality disagrees — which is to say, almost always.

The demo is the plan, the product is the execution

There is a reason this lesson is hard to learn, and it is the same reason the mistake is so common. Planning demos beautifully. A plan is a clean artifact — a list, a tree, a sequence — that a human can read and admire. It demonstrates intelligence in a form that is instantly recognizable. Execution, by contrast, is messy: it is tool calls returning partial data, it is format mismatches, it is re-plans, it is the agent deciding mid-task that its third assumption was wrong and the last two steps are garbage. None of that looks good in a demo. So teams optimize what demos well — the plan — and ship agents whose plans are gorgeous and whose executions are fragile. The product is not the plan. The product is everything that happens after the plan meets reality, which is to say, the product is the execution architecture.

The teams that figure this out stop showing the plan. They show traces. A trace is the actual record of what the agent did — the tools it called, the results it got, the moments it re-planned, the points where it recognized a dead end and turned around. A trace is ugly and specific and full of recovery, and it is a far better measure of an agent's quality than any plan will ever be. An agent with a mediocre plan and a brilliant execution architecture will outperform an agent with a brilliant plan and a brittle one, every time, because the real world does not care how elegant the prediction was. It cares whether the system can adapt when the prediction is wrong. And the prediction is always, eventually, wrong. The plan is not the work. The work is what the agent does when the plan breaks — and the plan always breaks.
