Every Queue Needs a Brake — Backpressure for Agents

Multi-agent systems rarely die of errors. They die of speed differentials: a fast producer, a slow consumer, an unbounded queue, and latency that compounds until every result arrives stale.

A planner decomposes a large goal into two hundred subtasks and dispatches them to a worker pool. The planner produces tasks a hundred times faster than the pool can finish them. Every unprocessed task lands in a queue with no maximum size. Twenty minutes in, the first results are coming back for work the planner has already revised twice. An hour in, workers are executing subtasks whose parent plan has been abandoned. The system reports ninety-seven percent completion — and produces nothing usable.

A producer ten times faster than its consumer, joined by a queue with no limit. Nothing crashes — the queue grows, latency compounds, and results describe a plan that no longer exists. The system just had no mechanism for the word 'enough'.

Queue depth is information

The instinctive fix is to cap the queue. Better than silence — but it treats the queue as a problem. The deeper truth is that queue depth is a measurement. A growing queue means the producer's model of the work is wrong. These are exactly the signals a control system wants, and they arrive for free.

An unbounded queue does not buy capacity. It buys time — and spends it on work that will be stale by the moment it finishes. Backpressure converts a speed differential from a silent failure into a steering signal.

Five things a pipeline can do when told to slow down

Backpressure is one word for a family of behaviors. Block: the producer waits. Shed: drop low-value tasks, publicly. Degrade: shrink the tasks themselves — cheaper models, shallower verification. Sample: process a fraction and extrapolate. Buffer externally: spill to durable storage with priorities. The best stacks classify work at dispatch and let the class choose the behavior.

Five overload behaviors, from crudest to most designed. Note what is not on the list: retrying. Under overload, a retry is not resilience — it is load wearing a costume.

Credit where credit is due

Credit-based flow control: the consumer decides how many tasks it can hold and grants exactly that many credits upstream. The planner may dispatch only against credits it holds. No unearned work can exist. The throttle moves with reality — fast workers return credits quickly; a stuck worker stops granting and the planner feels the friction within one round trip.

Credit-based flow control mapped onto a planner-and-pool system. Tasks and credits flow through the same channel in opposite directions. A stuck worker stops granting, and the planner slows without any monitoring infrastructure — TCP's window control for orchestration.

Faster models make the problem worse

It is tempting to believe faster inference will let the consumer catch up. The opposite happens: the planner fans out wider, decomposition gets deeper, and speed differentials migrate up the stack. Meanwhile the staleness threshold stays fixed, because it is set by the world. Bounded buffers, queue depth surfaced to the decider, explicit overload behavior per class of work, and credits instead of hope.
