The Eval Is the Spec: Your Test Suite Is Your Only Contract

No agent team ships against a written specification. There is exactly one artifact that describes what the system is supposed to do, executes on every change, and blocks the merge when the system disagrees: the eval suite. That makes it the specification — not metaphorically, but functionally.

Traditional software has types, contracts, tests, and a spec. Agent systems inherit almost none of this — behavior lives in a distribution, not a document. When intentions conflict, the conflict is resolved by whichever artifact actually runs in CI. Every other document is a wish. The suite is the contract.

This sounds like a platitude until you trace what it implies. If the eval suite is the spec, then the quality of the spec is the quality of the suite — and most suites are terrible in exactly the ways a spec written by an intern would be. They cover the demo's happy path, grade with a rubric a confident wrong answer can pass, and age invisibly while lending false confidence.

The chain of governance in an agent system. The only path from the agent to a ship decision runs through the eval suite — which means the suite, not the docs, defines what the system is required to be.

An eval is a clause, and most clauses are vague

Strip an eval down and it has four parts: task, environment, rubric, threshold. Most teams obsess over the task and sleep on the other three — backwards, because the rubric and threshold are where the contract's teeth are. A task with a rubric of 'the code looks better' is not a clause; it is a vibe.

The strong pattern is a ladder of graders: deterministic checks wherever possible, model-graded rubrics for judgment calls, humans for taste. The ladder is a specification written in three formalisms — and the deterministic floor is the only part that never argues back. Everything pushed to a deterministic check becomes law; everything left to model grading stays case law.

Every eval's rubric is a ladder. Deterministic checks are statute law, model-graded rubrics are case law, human graders are constitutional review. The discipline is migration: push every behavior as far down the ladder as it will go.

A grader you have not adversarially tested is a clause you have not read. If a confident wrong answer cannot fail your rubric, the rubric is the bug — and it ships with every merge.

Spec rot, and the flywheel that prevents it

The failure that ends careers is not the bad eval — it is the stale one. Every case passes, dashboards are green, while users encounter situations the suite has never heard of — and the agent's behavior there is, from the contract's point of view, undefined. Green dashboards over unspecified territory are the most dangerous artifact in the field.

The fix is mechanical: production traces flow back into the suite. Record every run, mine the traces for novel situations and graded failures, distill them into new cases, and let each case permanently shrink the territory of undefined behavior. A team running this loop has a spec that converges toward its users. One that doesn't has a spec that converges toward its own demo.

The flywheel that keeps the spec alive. Traces are mined for situations the suite has never seen; each becomes a permanent case; undefined territory shrinks with every rotation.

None of this requires new infrastructure so much as a new posture. The eval suite is a legal document: version it and diff it like code, give every case a provenance, adversarially test the graders, and update the suite — not the prompt — first after every incident.

An agent team's real product is two artifacts: the system, and the specification that governs it. Teams that accept this stop arguing about what the agent 'should' do and start arguing about specific cases with specific graders — disputes that end, because the winner encodes their position in a clause that runs forever. The contract was always there. The only question is whether it was written on purpose.
