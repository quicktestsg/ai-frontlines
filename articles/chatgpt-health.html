ChatGPT Health Rolls Out to All U.S. Users

Apple Health integration, connected medical records, and 300 million weekly health questions — OpenAI's most consequential consumer play yet.

OpenAI has quietly been running one of the largest unintentional health platforms on the planet. The company says 300 million people ask ChatGPT health questions every week — a number that would make it, by some measures, a larger health-information surface than WebMD, the Mayo Clinic's patient portal, or most national telehealth services combined. The new ChatGPT Health rollout, now reaching all U.S. users, is OpenAI's attempt to take that informal behavior seriously and turn it into a structured product.

The mechanics are straightforward but politically loaded. Users can now connect Apple Health data and supported medical records, giving the model persistent context about their vitals, lab results, medications, and conditions. That context flows through the latest models — GPT-5.5 Instant first brought health intelligence to free users, and GPT-5.6 Sol builds on the foundation with richer reasoning over clinical data. OpenAI says it worked with hundreds of physicians to shape the feature's clinical grounding and safety behavior, a process that visibly shaped the product's cautious tone.

Three hundred million weekly health questions is not a feature request — it's a de facto healthcare system that was never designed as one. OpenAI's choice is whether to formalize that role or let it stay accidental.

What makes this rollout defensible — and what will be scrutinized hardest — is the data treatment. OpenAI states plainly that connected records and Apple Health data will not be used to train foundation models or target advertising. That's a meaningful commitment, and one the company will need to keep auditable as health systems, insurers, and regulators inevitably come asking questions. The healthcare industry has a long, well-documented history of data leakage through analytics partners and third-party SDKs; the bar for "trust us" is therefore not high, it's subterranean.

The bigger strategic question is where ChatGPT Health sits in the care continuum. OpenAI has been careful to frame it as informational, not diagnostic — but the line between "helping me understand my lab results" and "advising me on what to do about them" dissolves quickly in practice, especially for users without a strong primary-care relationship. The U.S. healthcare access crisis means millions of people will use this feature precisely because they can't reach a doctor easily. That's the real market, and the real responsibility. The product is good enough that it will change behavior. The question is whether OpenAI is ready for what that behavior change actually means.
